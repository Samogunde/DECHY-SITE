Here is what a healthy first month looks like, week by week, and what the client should do.

## Week 1: Kickoff and alignment

**What the team does:**
- Holds a kickoff meeting with everyone involved
- Reviews your brief, goals and constraints
- Interviews stakeholders and, where possible, real users
- Agrees on how you'll communicate: tools, meeting times and response expectations
- Sets up project tools: task board, shared documents, chat and a shared drive
- Confirms the success measures for the project
- Identifies risks and unknowns

**What you should do:**
- Name one decision-maker on your side
- Share everything relevant: documents, brand files, existing systems, competitor examples
- Give access to any systems the team needs to see
- Be clear about priorities, budget limits and hard deadlines
- Introduce the team to the people who will use the product

**Deliverable at the end of week 1:** a written summary of goals, scope and the plan for the next three weeks.

## Week 2: Discovery and planning

**What the team does:**
- Maps the user journeys and the key flows
- Turns requirements into a prioritized list of features
- Separates the must-haves from the nice-to-haves
- Produces the first version of the technical plan: architecture, technologies and integrations
- Breaks the work into phases and sprints
- Updates the estimate, if the picture has changed

**What you should do:**
- Answer questions quickly. This is where delays begin.
- Challenge the feature list: is each item necessary for the first release?
- Confirm the priorities
- Review and approve the plan

**Deliverable at the end of week 2:** an approved scope for the first release, a roadmap with milestones and a clear list of what is not included.

## Week 3: Design and foundations

**What the team does:**
- Creates wireframes of the key screens, then visual designs
- Builds a clickable prototype, so you can try the flow before any code is written
- Sets up the technical foundations: code repository, development and test environments, automated checks and deployment pipeline
- Starts the basic building blocks, such as login, navigation and the data structure
- Begins security and access setup

**What you should do:**
- Review designs promptly and give specific feedback: "this button is hard to find" is better than "I don't like it"
- Test the prototype with a few real users, if you can
- Confirm that code repositories and accounts are in your name
- Provide content and brand assets that were requested

**Deliverable at the end of week 3:** approved designs for the main screens and a working development environment.

## Week 4: First working software

**What the team does:**
- Builds the first thin slice of real functionality, from the screen through to the database
- Runs the first demo of working software, even if rough
- Tests what's been built
- Reviews progress against the plan
- Adjusts the plan based on what it has learned

**What you should do:**
- Attend the demo and try the product yourself
- Give feedback in writing, listing what works and what must change
- Review the budget and schedule status
- Confirm priorities for the next phase

**Deliverable at the end of week 4:** a working early version you can click through, plus an updated plan and a status report.

## What good communication looks like

- A **short weekly meeting** on progress, risks and decisions
- A **written weekly update** summarizing what was done, what is next and what is blocked
- A **shared task board** you can see at any time
- **Quick responses**, within one working day for normal questions
- **Early warnings.** Problems are raised as soon as they are known.

## Signs the start is going well

- You can describe the project goals in one sentence, and so can the team
- Scope is written down and agreed
- You know who is working on your project and what each person does
- Decisions are recorded
- You have seen designs and a first demo
- The team asks sharp questions and pushes back when something doesn't make sense
- Accounts and code are in your name

## Warning signs

- Weeks pass with no visible output
- The team avoids showing work in progress
- Answers to simple questions are vague
- Scope keeps changing without a record
- You've never spoken to the people doing the work
- Accounts and code sit under the vendor's name
- The plan has no dates and no milestones

If you see these in the first month, raise them straight away. Early course correction is cheap.

## Your own checklist for the first 30 days

**Before the kickoff:**
- [ ] One-page brief shared
- [ ] Decision-maker named
- [ ] Brand assets and content gathered
- [ ] System access prepared
- [ ] Budget and deadline confirmed

**During the month:**
- [ ] Attend the kickoff and weekly meetings
- [ ] Respond to questions within a day
- [ ] Review designs with specific feedback
- [ ] Set up accounts in your name
- [ ] Attend the first demo
- [ ] Review budget and schedule

**At the end of the month:**
- [ ] Scope for the first release approved
- [ ] Roadmap with milestones received
- [ ] Designs approved
- [ ] Working early version seen
- [ ] Risks and next steps understood

## Common mistakes by clients

- **Disappearing after kickoff,** and returning only at the end
- **Slow feedback,** which delays the whole schedule
- **Changing priorities weekly**
- **Sending feedback from many people** without a single owner
- **Waiting to share content,** which holds up design and development
- **Not looking at demos carefully,** so problems surface late

## Frequently asked questions

### What happens in the first week of a software project?
Kickoff, goal alignment, access setup, project tools and a plan for the next few weeks.

### When will I see working software?
A good team shows first working pieces within the first four to six weeks, often earlier for small projects.

### What does the client need to do at the start?
Name a decision-maker, share information and assets, give access to systems, respond quickly and review designs and plans.

### How long does discovery take?
Usually one to three weeks for a focused project, longer for large or complex systems.

### What if I'm not happy with early progress?
Say so early and specifically. Ask for a short review, adjust the plan and agree on what changes. Early adjustments cost little.

### Should I see designs before development starts?
Yes. Approved designs and a clickable prototype reduce misunderstandings and rework.
