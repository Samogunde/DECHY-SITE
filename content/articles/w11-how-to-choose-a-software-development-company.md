Picking the wrong partner is expensive. It usually costs months, not just money. This guide gives you a clear process, so you can decide with confidence.

## Step 1: Know what you need before you shop

Write one page covering:

- The problem the software solves and who uses it
- The three most important features
- Your budget range and your target launch date
- Any systems it must connect to
- Anything you already have: designs, documents, a prototype

You don't need a technical spec. You do need clarity. Agencies respond better to clear briefs, and you will notice quickly who asks sharp questions and who just sends a price.

## Step 2: Build a shortlist of three to five

Look for companies that have done work like yours, in your industry or with a similar level of complexity. Good places to start:

- Recommendations from people in your network
- Review platforms such as Clutch and GoodFirms
- Search results for the kind of project you need
- Portfolios and case studies on their websites

Avoid long lists. Three to five serious conversations beat fifteen shallow ones.

## Step 3: Check the proof

**Case studies.** Look for real problems, real results and named outcomes. Vague claims like "we helped a client grow" are weak. A good case study explains the situation, what was built and what changed.

**Reviews.** Read both the best and the worst ones. Look at how the company responds to criticism.

**References.** Ask for two or three past clients you can speak to. A confident agency will say yes. Ask the clients how the team handled problems, not just whether the project went well.

**Technical fit.** Ask which technologies they use and why. A team that recommends the same stack for every problem may be selling its habits, not your solution.

## Step 4: Meet the real team

Many projects go wrong because the people in the sales meeting are not the people doing the work.

Ask:

- Who will be my project manager?
- Who are the lead developer and designer?
- Will I speak to them directly?
- What happens if someone leaves the project?

Look at how the team communicates in the first calls. Response speed, clarity and honesty at this stage predict the whole project.

## Step 5: Understand how they work

A reliable team has a clear process. Ask them to describe:

- How they plan and estimate work
- How often you will see progress, and in what form
- How they test before delivery
- How changes are handled
- What tools they use for tracking tasks and communication

Good teams show work early and often, usually every one to two weeks. Teams that disappear for two months and then deliver a "big reveal" cause the worst surprises.

## Step 6: Compare proposals the right way

Lay the proposals side by side and compare:

| What to check | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Scope and what is excluded | The cheapest quote often leaves things out |
| Team size and roles | A senior-light team may deliver slower or weaker work |
| Timeline with milestones | Milestones make progress measurable |
| Testing and quality checks | Missing testing means hidden cost later |
| Post-launch support | Software needs care after release |
| Change process | Every project changes |
| Total cost and payment schedule | Pay against delivered milestones, not time alone |

Do not choose on price alone. A quote that is 40 percent below the others usually means less scope, a junior team or a plan to recover margin through change requests.

## Step 7: Read the contract

Before you sign, make sure the contract says:

- **You own the code, designs and accounts** from the moment you pay
- **How payments are tied to milestones**
- **What counts as acceptance** of each deliverable
- **How changes and extra work are priced**
- **Confidentiality** of your idea and data
- **What happens at the end:** handover of code, documentation and access
- **Support or warranty** after launch

If an agency holds your code hostage or resists handing over accounts, walk away.

## Red flags

- They give a precise price before asking you a single question
- They promise unrealistic speed
- They say yes to everything and never push back
- No clear project manager
- They cannot show similar work or refuse to share references
- Vague contract with no ownership clause
- Pressure to sign quickly
- Heavy discount that expires today

## Green flags

- They ask hard questions about your users and goals
- They recommend a smaller first version
- They explain trade-offs in plain language
- They show you who will work on the project
- They are open about risks and unknowns
- They offer a paid discovery phase to reduce uncertainty
- They talk about life after launch without being asked

## A simple way to decide

Score each finalist from 1 to 5 on these six points:

1. Relevant experience
2. Communication and honesty
3. Team quality
4. Process and transparency
5. Value for the scope
6. Trust and chemistry

Trust and chemistry count. You will work with this team for months, and hard days will come. Choose people you can talk to honestly.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many companies should I talk to?
Three to five. That is enough to compare approaches and prices without wasting weeks.

### Should I choose the cheapest software development company?
Not by default. Compare scope, team and process. A very low price often means fewer features, junior staff or hidden extras later.

### Is it better to choose a local or an offshore company?
Both can work. Teams in your time zone make meetings easier. Offshore teams can offer strong value and extended working hours. Choose on communication, process and proof of results, not on location alone.

### What should I ask a software development company in the first call?
Ask about similar projects, who will work on yours, how they handle changes, how they test and what support looks like after launch. You'll find more questions in our [15 questions to ask before hiring an agency](/resources/guides/questions-to-ask-a-software-development-company/).

### How long does it take to choose a development partner?
Two to four weeks is realistic for a serious project. Rushing is risky, but dragging it out costs time too.

### What if I'm not technical?
You don't have to be. Good agencies translate technical choices into business terms. If they hide behind jargon, treat it as a warning sign.
