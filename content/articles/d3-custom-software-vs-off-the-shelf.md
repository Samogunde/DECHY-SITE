## What the two options mean

Off-the-shelf software is a finished product built for many businesses at once. You sign up, configure it, and start using it. Most products today are software as a service (SaaS), meaning you pay a subscription and the vendor hosts and updates the software for you.

Custom software is built for one organization. It is designed around how that organization actually works: its steps, its rules, its customers, and its data. You own it, and you decide how it changes.

Neither is better in general. Each is the right answer to a different kind of problem. For a fuller decision method that also covers connecting your existing tools, read our guide on [whether to build, buy, or integrate](/resources/guides/build-buy-or-integrate-business-software/).

## Cost over time, not just at the start

Off-the-shelf software looks cheaper, and at the start it usually is. There is no build to pay for. You pay a monthly or yearly fee and begin.

Over several years, the picture can change. Per-user fees grow as the team grows. Advanced features often sit behind higher tiers. Workarounds take staff time every day, and that time rarely appears in a budget. Data can be hard to move out if you decide to switch.

Custom software costs more up front because it has to be designed and built. After that, the main costs are hosting, support, and improvements. There are no per-user fees, so growth does not raise the price of the software itself.

The fair comparison is the total cost over several years, including staff time saved or lost. Our guide to [what custom software costs](/resources/cost-guides/how-much-does-custom-software-cost/) shows typical ranges.

- Off-the-shelf: low start, costs grow with users and tiers
- Custom: higher start, then hosting, support, and improvements
- Compare the full cost over several years

## Fit with the way you work

A ready-made product is designed for the average business. If your process is close to average, that is ideal. You benefit from features and lessons drawn from many other companies.

If your process is distinctive, the product forces you to change. Sometimes that change is healthy and removes old habits. Often it is not. Staff build spreadsheets and side processes to fill the gaps, and the work that customers valued most becomes harder.

Custom software fits the process instead. Screens show what each person needs. Steps follow your rules. That fit is the main reason to build.

## Control, ownership, and risk

With off-the-shelf software, the vendor decides what changes and when. A feature you rely on can be changed, moved to a higher tier, or removed. Prices can rise. If the vendor is acquired or closes, you may need to move quickly.

With custom software, you control the roadmap. You own the code and the data, so any capable team can maintain it. The risk shifts to delivery: the software has to be built well and looked after. Choosing a team that works in stages, shows working software early, and documents everything keeps that risk low.

## Speed

Off-the-shelf software wins on speed to start. A product can be running in days.

Custom software takes longer, but less than many expect. A focused first version that solves the most painful step can often be delivered in weeks, then grown in stages. The value starts arriving before the whole system is finished.

## The common middle path

Most real decisions are not all-or-nothing. A business might keep its accounting package and its customer relationship management (CRM) system, connect them so information flows between them, and build a small custom tool for the one process that makes it different.

That combination keeps the strengths of proven products, removes retyping between systems, and protects what is distinctive. It is the most common outcome when a decision is made carefully.

## Questions to ask before you decide

Before you compare prices, answer a few questions in writing. They make the right choice much clearer.

What exactly is the work this software has to support, step by step? Who uses it, and how often? Which parts of the process do customers notice? Which tools do you already own, and do they hold the right information?

Then look at the products on the market with those answers in hand. Count the workarounds each one would need. Check how you would get your data out if you left. Ask what the price would be with twice as many users.

Finally, estimate what a focused custom first version would cover. If a product fits with few workarounds, buy it. If every product breaks the part that matters most, build that part.

- Describe the work step by step before looking at products
- Count the workarounds each product would need
- Check data export and pricing at twice your size
- Build only where products break what matters most

## A worked example

Picture a specialist manufacturer that quotes complex custom orders. Quoting depends on materials, machine time, and rules that only its senior staff fully understand. Several quoting products were tried. Each forced a simpler model, and staff went back to spreadsheets.

The manufacturer keeps its accounting system and CRM, because both work well. It builds a custom quoting tool that captures its own rules, connects to the CRM for customer details, and sends accepted quotes to accounting. The first release covers the most common order types. The rest follow in stages.

## Common questions

### Is custom software always more expensive?

It costs more at the start. Over several years it can cost less, especially when per-user fees are high or workarounds waste a lot of staff time.

### Can we customize an off-the-shelf product instead?

Often, yes, within the limits the vendor allows. Heavy customization can become fragile when the vendor updates the product, so it is worth weighing against a small custom build.

### How do we know if our process is distinctive enough?

Ask whether customers would notice if the process changed. If the way you quote, schedule, deliver, or report is part of why customers choose you, it is worth protecting.

### Who looks after custom software once it is built?

You decide. We can look after it, your own team can, or another provider can, because you own the code and the documentation.

### Can we start with a product and build later?

Yes. Many businesses start with a product, learn what they need, and build once the gaps are clear.
