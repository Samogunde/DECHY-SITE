Here is a roadmap with the stages in order.

## Stage 1: Find a problem worth solving

The best SaaS ideas come from problems you can see up close.

- **Start with a specific person.** "Small accounting firms with 3 to 10 staff" is a market. "Businesses" is not.
- **Look for a painful, frequent, expensive problem.** People pay to remove problems that cost them time, money or stress every week.
- **Look for existing workarounds.** Spreadsheets, email chains, manual processes and clumsy tools signal demand.
- **Check whether people already pay.** Competitors with customers prove the market. Lack of any competitor can mean no demand.

Ask yourself: would these people pay for this, and how much?

## Stage 2: Validate before you build

Spend a few weeks, not months, testing the idea.

1. **Talk to 20 target customers.** Ask how they handle the problem today, what it costs them and what they've tried. Listen for strong emotion and specific stories.
2. **Test willingness to pay.** Ask: "If this existed, would you pay $X a month?" Better still, take deposits or signed letters of intent.
3. **Build a simple landing page.** Describe the problem and the solution, then measure sign-ups.
4. **Try a manual version.** Solve the problem by hand for three to five customers. If they value it, you've validated demand and learned what to build.

If you can't get ten people excited, the product isn't ready to build.

## Stage 3: Define your MVP

Your first version does one thing well.

- **The core job:** the single outcome customers pay for
- **Essential features:** only what the core job needs
- **What to skip:** advanced settings, integrations, reports, mobile apps and anything people can do manually at first

Typical SaaS MVP essentials:

- Sign-up and login
- The core workflow
- A simple dashboard
- Basic billing
- Basic admin tools so you can help customers
- Email notifications

See our guide on [building an MVP](/resources/cost-guides/how-much-does-it-cost-to-build-an-mvp/) for budgeting detail.

## Stage 4: Choose your approach to building

**Build it yourself** if you are technical. It keeps costs low and speeds learning.

**Hire a freelancer** for a small, well-defined MVP.

**Hire an agency or dedicated team** if you need design, development and testing together, and you want predictable delivery.

**No-code tools** can validate simple ideas quickly, but custom logic, scale and unique experiences usually need real code.

Whichever route you choose:

- Own your code and accounts
- Use common, proven technology
- Plan for subscriptions, user accounts and security from the beginning, since they are harder to add later
- Keep documentation, so you aren't locked in

## Stage 5: Set your pricing early

Many founders delay pricing. Don't. Charging early is the strongest validation.

- **Price by value, not by cost.** If you save a customer 10 hours a month, they'll pay far more than your hosting bill.
- **Keep it simple.** Two or three plans at most.
- **Pick a clear pricing metric,** such as per user, per project or per usage level.
- **Offer an annual option** at a discount, which improves cash flow.
- **Start higher than feels comfortable.** It is easier to discount than to raise prices later.
- **Avoid a permanent free plan** early on. A time-limited trial or a paid pilot is better.

Early customers can have a discounted "founder" price in exchange for feedback and testimonials.

## Stage 6: Launch and get your first ten customers

Your first customers come from direct effort.

- **Go back to the people you interviewed.** They already care.
- **Reach out personally.** Email, LinkedIn and direct messages to ideal customers. Explain the problem, not your features.
- **Join communities** where your target customers gather, and be useful, not promotional.
- **Ask for introductions.** Every happy early customer knows others like them.
- **Offer hands-on onboarding.** Help each customer get set up personally.

At this stage, you are learning as much as selling. Take notes on every conversation.

## Stage 7: Customers 10 to 50

Now you look for patterns.

- **Which customers get the most value, and what do they have in common?** Focus on those.
- **Where do people drop off?** Fix the onboarding.
- **What do customers ask for most?** Build that first.
- **Why do some cancel?** Ask every one of them.

Add simple analytics to track sign-ups, activation, usage and cancellations.

Begin repeatable channels:

- **Content and search:** articles that answer your customers' questions
- **Partnerships:** tools and consultants who serve the same customers
- **Directories and marketplaces** where buyers look for solutions
- **Referrals:** a simple reward for customers who introduce others
- **Outbound:** a consistent, targeted weekly routine

## Stage 8: Customers 50 to 100

- **Improve activation:** the share of new users who reach their first success quickly
- **Reduce churn:** talk to customers at risk, fix recurring issues, add the features that keep people
- **Build proof:** case studies, testimonials and reviews
- **Tighten the message:** use your customers' own words on your website
- **Add the important missing features,** based on real requests
- **Set up proper support:** documentation, a help email and a way to track issues

## Metrics to watch

| Metric | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Sign-ups per week | Interest |
| Activation rate | Whether new users reach value |
| Trial-to-paid conversion | Whether people will pay |
| Monthly recurring revenue (MRR) | Business size |
| Churn rate | How many customers leave |
| Customer acquisition cost | What it costs to win a customer |
| Customer lifetime value | What a customer is worth |
| Net promoter or satisfaction score | Happiness |

If lifetime value is not well above acquisition cost, growth will be expensive.

## Common mistakes

- **Building for a year before showing anyone.** Show it early and often.
- **Too many features, too early.** Each one adds cost and complexity.
- **No clear customer.** "Everyone" is not a target.
- **Free forever.** People don't value what they don't pay for.
- **Ignoring onboarding.** If users don't succeed in the first session, many never return.
- **Skipping security and backups.** One incident can destroy trust.
- **Growing before retention is healthy.** Pouring marketing money into a leaky product wastes it.
- **Doing it all alone, with no feedback.**

## A realistic timeline

| Stage | Typical time |
|---|---|
| Problem and validation | 3 to 6 weeks |
| MVP design and build | 8 to 16 weeks |
| Private beta and first customers | 4 to 8 weeks |
| Customers 10 to 50 | 3 to 6 months |
| Customers 50 to 100 | 3 to 6 months |

Timelines vary widely by market, price and effort.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does it cost to build a SaaS product?
A custom SaaS MVP typically costs {{fact:price.mvp-saas-mvp-with-billing}}. Simpler versions can be done for less, and complex ones for more.

### How long does it take to get the first hundred customers?
Often 6 to 18 months, depending on price, market and effort. Higher-priced products reach a hundred customers more slowly than low-priced ones.

### Should I charge from day one?
Yes, if you can. Paying customers give the clearest feedback. Offer founder pricing to early adopters.

### Should I build a mobile app for my SaaS?
Usually not at first. A good responsive web app is enough for most SaaS products at the start.

### How do I get my first customers?
Personal outreach to people you've already spoken to, communities where your customers gather and referrals from early users.

### Do I need a technical co-founder?
Not necessarily. You can work with an agency or freelancers, but you need someone who can make technology decisions and own the product direction.
