This is general information to help you read a contract, not legal advice. For any significant project, have a qualified lawyer review the agreement, especially if the parties are in different countries.

## 1. The parties and the structure

- [ ] Full legal names and addresses of both sides
- [ ] Which documents make up the agreement, for example a master agreement plus a statement of work for each project
- [ ] Which law governs the contract, and where disputes are resolved
- [ ] The order of priority if documents conflict

Using a master agreement with separate statements of work makes it easy to add phases later without renegotiating everything.

## 2. Scope of work

This is the heart of the contract.

- [ ] A clear description of what will be delivered: features, screens, user roles and integrations
- [ ] An explicit list of what is **not** included
- [ ] The platforms and technologies to be used
- [ ] The assumptions made, such as "client provides all content" or "two rounds of design revisions"
- [ ] The tools the team will use, and who pays for them
- [ ] Reference to the designs, specifications or prototype that define the work

If it is not written down, it is not in scope. Most disputes come from vague scope.

## 3. Timeline and milestones

- [ ] Start date and target delivery dates
- [ ] Milestones with clear deliverables
- [ ] What happens if either side causes a delay
- [ ] Dependencies, such as "the client will provide access to X by date Y"
- [ ] A process for adjusting the schedule when scope changes

Dates in a contract are targets that depend on both sides doing their part.

## 4. Price and payment

- [ ] The pricing model: fixed price, time and materials or monthly retainer
- [ ] Total price or estimate, and what it covers
- [ ] A cap or a budget alert, for time-and-materials work
- [ ] Payment schedule tied to milestones or delivered work
- [ ] A reasonable upfront deposit, not a large share of the total
- [ ] Invoice terms: due dates, currency, taxes and payment method
- [ ] Late payment terms
- [ ] How expenses and third-party costs are handled
- [ ] Rates for extra work

See our guide on [pricing models](/resources/cost-guides/fixed-price-vs-time-and-materials-vs-dedicated-team/).

## 5. Acceptance and quality

- [ ] How each deliverable is reviewed and accepted
- [ ] A defined review period, for example 5 to 10 working days
- [ ] Criteria for "done," such as meeting the written requirements and passing agreed tests
- [ ] How defects are reported and fixed during acceptance
- [ ] What happens if the work fails acceptance repeatedly
- [ ] Standards for testing and quality

Without acceptance criteria, "finished" becomes a matter of opinion.

## 6. Changes to scope

- [ ] A written change request process
- [ ] Each change is described, estimated for cost and time and approved by both sides before work begins
- [ ] Who has authority to approve changes on each side
- [ ] How changes affect the schedule and payments

A simple, fair change process protects you from surprise invoices and protects the team from endless extras.

## 7. Intellectual property and ownership

This is the clause that matters most when something goes wrong.

- [ ] You own the custom code, designs, documentation and other deliverables, from the moment you pay or on completion, as agreed
- [ ] The vendor assigns rights in writing, rather than merely licensing them
- [ ] Third-party and open-source components are identified, with their licenses
- [ ] The vendor keeps rights to its pre-existing tools and general know-how, with you receiving a license to use anything of theirs that is embedded in your product
- [ ] Repositories, hosting, domains, app store and cloud accounts are in your name
- [ ] You receive source code, documentation and credentials at handover

If a contract says you only get a license to the code you paid to build, question it.

## 8. Confidentiality and data protection

- [ ] Both sides keep each other's confidential information private
- [ ] Rules on how your data and your customers' data are handled and stored
- [ ] Security practices the vendor will follow
- [ ] Compliance with laws that apply, such as GDPR (the EU's data protection law)
- [ ] A data processing agreement, where personal data is involved
- [ ] What happens to your data when the contract ends
- [ ] Requirement to notify you promptly of any security incident

## 9. Team and subcontractors

- [ ] Key people named, with their roles
- [ ] Notice and handover if a key person is replaced
- [ ] Approval needed before the work is passed to subcontractors
- [ ] The vendor remains responsible for subcontractors' work
- [ ] Exclusivity or non-solicitation clauses are reasonable, if present

## 10. Warranty and support

- [ ] A warranty period, often 30 to 90 days, during which defects are fixed at no charge
- [ ] A clear definition of a defect versus a new feature request
- [ ] Ongoing support and maintenance terms, if any: response times, hours and cost
- [ ] Service levels for critical issues
- [ ] What is excluded from the warranty, such as problems caused by third-party changes

## 11. Liability and insurance

- [ ] A limit on each side's liability, commonly linked to the fees paid
- [ ] Exclusions for things such as breach of confidentiality or intellectual property infringement, which are often treated separately
- [ ] The vendor holds appropriate professional and cyber insurance
- [ ] The vendor confirms the work will not infringe third-party rights

Liability terms are an area where legal advice is worth the cost.

## 12. Termination and exit

- [ ] How either side can end the contract, and how much notice is needed
- [ ] Rights to terminate for serious breach
- [ ] What you pay for if the contract ends early: work done to date
- [ ] You receive all work completed so far, including code and documentation, even on early termination
- [ ] A handover process, including a walkthrough and access to all accounts
- [ ] Return or deletion of data and confidential material

A good exit clause is a sign of a confident, fair partner.

## 13. Dispute resolution

- [ ] A first step of talking things through between senior people
- [ ] Mediation or arbitration before court, where suitable
- [ ] The governing law and the location of any proceedings

## 14. Practical checks before signing

- [ ] You understand every clause, or you have asked for it to be explained
- [ ] The scope matches your brief and the proposal
- [ ] The price and payment terms match what you were told
- [ ] The names and dates are correct
- [ ] A lawyer has reviewed it, for projects of meaningful size
- [ ] Both sides sign, and you keep a full copy

## Warning signs

- The vendor keeps the code until the final payment, or only licenses it to you
- Large upfront payments with no milestones
- No acceptance process
- Unlimited fees for changes with no approval step
- No exit or handover clause
- Vendor-owned accounts for your hosting and app stores
- Unusually broad non-compete or exclusivity terms
- A refusal to adjust anything

## Frequently asked questions

### Who should own the code in a software development contract?
You, in most cases. The contract should assign the rights in the custom code and deliverables to you, with any third-party or open-source parts clearly listed.

### What is a statement of work?
A document that describes a specific project: scope, deliverables, timeline, price and assumptions. It sits under a master agreement that holds the general terms.

### How much should I pay upfront?
A modest deposit is normal, often 10 to 30 percent. The rest should follow delivered milestones that you've reviewed.

### Do I need a lawyer to review the contract?
For any project of meaningful size, yes. The cost is small compared with the risk of a bad contract.

### What if the agency won't change the contract?
Ask why. Reasonable partners explain their position and often compromise on fair points. A flat refusal to discuss ownership or exit terms is a reason to walk away.

### What should happen to my data when the contract ends?
It should be returned to you in a usable format, and then deleted from the vendor's systems, with confirmation in writing.
