Here are ten warning signs, why each matters and what to ask.

## 1. A vague scope

**What it looks like:** "Develop a mobile app with all required features." "Build a platform for managing customers." No list of screens, features or user roles.

**Why it matters:** vague scope is how projects grow without a price. The agency later says each detail you assumed was included is extra.

**Ask:** "Can you list every feature, screen and user type that is included, and everything that is not?"

## 2. No list of exclusions

**What it looks like:** the proposal says what you get, never what you don't get.

**Why it matters:** hidden exclusions are the most common source of surprise invoices. Content creation, third-party fees, extra design rounds, data migration and hosting are often left out.

**Ask:** "What is explicitly out of scope, and what would those items cost?"

## 3. No testing, QA or project management

**What it looks like:** the budget covers design and development only.

**Why it matters:** testing and management are 20 to 30 percent of real project effort. If they aren't priced, they aren't happening, and you will find the bugs yourself.

**Ask:** "What testing do you do, who does it and what is the cost of it?"

## 4. A price far below every other quote

**What it looks like:** one quote at half the price of the other three.

**Why it matters:** there are only a few ways to be that cheap: less scope, a junior team, cut testing, or a plan to recover margin through change requests. Software is labour, and labour has a real cost.

**Ask:** "Why is your price lower than others? What assumptions does it make?" Get the answer in writing.

## 5. An unrealistic timeline

**What it looks like:** a complex platform in four weeks. "We can start tomorrow and launch in a month."

**Why it matters:** the agency wants to win, or has not understood the work. Rushed projects skip steps and arrive late anyway.

**Ask:** "Walk me through the timeline by phase. What would cause delays?" Compare it with other quotes and your own sense of the work.

## 6. No clarity on who owns the code

**What it looks like:** silence on ownership, or terms saying the agency keeps rights until final payment or licenses the code to you.

**Why it matters:** if you don't own the code, you can't move to another team, and the agency has leverage over you.

**Ask:** "Do I own all source code, designs and accounts from the date I pay? Will repositories and hosting be set up in my name?"

## 7. No named team or a bait-and-switch

**What it looks like:** "our experienced team will handle it" with no names, roles or seniority. Senior people in the sales meeting, juniors on the project.

**Why it matters:** quality comes from the actual people doing the work.

**Ask:** "Who exactly will work on my project, in what role and for how many hours a week? Can I meet them before I sign?"

## 8. No plan for after launch

**What it looks like:** the proposal ends at delivery. No mention of bug fixes, support, hosting or maintenance.

**Why it matters:** software needs care after release. Without a plan, you can be left with a system nobody knows how to fix.

**Ask:** "What happens in the first 30 days after launch? Is there a warranty period? What does ongoing support cost?"

## 9. No change process

**What it looks like:** no mention of how new requests are handled, or "all changes are billed hourly" with no approval step.

**Why it matters:** every project changes. Without a process, you will argue about every change, or get billed without knowing.

**Ask:** "How are changes requested, estimated and approved? Will I see the cost before work starts?"

## 10. Pressure and gimmicks

**What it looks like:** a "discount expires Friday." Pushing you to sign before you've compared options. Large upfront payments with little to show.

**Why it matters:** a good agency is comfortable with you taking time. High pressure suggests they need the sale more than you need the product.

**Ask:** "Can I have two weeks to review this? Can payments be tied to milestones instead of dates?"

## Bonus warning signs

- **They never push back.** If every idea is "great" and nothing is questioned, they aren't thinking critically.
- **No questions asked.** A proposal produced without asking about users, goals or constraints is a template.
- **Reluctance to share references.** Confident agencies connect you with past clients.
- **Heavy jargon.** If you can't understand the proposal, ask for plain language. Good agencies translate.
- **A payment schedule that front-loads money.** Large upfront payments reduce your leverage.

## Green flags to look for instead

- Questions about your users, goals and constraints
- A recommendation to start with a smaller first version
- A clear list of assumptions and risks
- Named people, roles and time commitments
- Milestone-based payments
- Honest talk about what could go wrong
- A defined handover and support plan

## A quick proposal checklist

Before signing, make sure the proposal covers:

- [ ] Detailed scope and exclusions
- [ ] Assumptions in writing
- [ ] Team members and roles
- [ ] Timeline with milestones
- [ ] Price breakdown
- [ ] Testing and QA
- [ ] Payment tied to milestones
- [ ] Change process
- [ ] Ownership of code and accounts
- [ ] Post-launch support or warranty

If any box is empty, ask for it to be added.

## What to do if you spot red flags

1. **Ask directly.** Raise the concern and see how they respond. Defensive replies are a signal.
2. **Request a revised proposal.** Good agencies will clarify and improve it.
3. **Compare with other quotes.** Differences show where something is missing.
4. **Get a second opinion.** A technical advisor can review the proposal in an hour or two.
5. **Walk away if needed.** A bad start rarely improves.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should a software proposal include?
Scope and exclusions, assumptions, team, timeline, price breakdown, testing, payment terms, change process, ownership terms and post-launch support.

### Is the cheapest proposal a red flag?
Not always, but a price far below the others deserves a close look. Ask what is missing and get the assumptions in writing.

### How long should I take to review a proposal?
Take a week or two. Anyone who pressures you to decide in a day is not helping you.

### Should payments be upfront?
A modest deposit is normal. The rest should be tied to delivered milestones that you have reviewed.

### Who should own the source code?
You. The contract should say you own the code, designs and accounts from the moment you pay.

### Can I ask for a proposal review?
Yes, and you should if you aren't technical. Send us the proposal and we'll tell you honestly what looks sound and what doesn't.
