Studies of software projects often find that a large share run late, over budget or fail to deliver the expected value. Exact numbers vary by study and definition, but the pattern is consistent. Here are the main causes and what to do about each.

## 1. No clear goal

**What happens:** the project starts with "we need an app" or "we need a new system." Nobody can say what success looks like. Features are built, but nobody can tell whether they work.

**How to avoid it:**
- Write one sentence describing the problem and the outcome you want
- Set two or three measurable targets, for example "reduce booking time to 2 minutes"
- Share the goal with everyone on the project, including the team building it
- Check each feature against the goal

## 2. Scope creep

**What happens:** requests pile up, "just one more feature," "while you're at it." The budget and schedule were set for the original scope, so they slip, quality drops or both.

**How to avoid it:**
- Agree on a written scope with a clear "not included" list
- Keep a backlog of ideas for later, separate from the current build
- Use a simple change process: describe, estimate, approve, then build
- Start with a minimum version, and add features after launch

## 3. Unrealistic budget or timeline

**What happens:** the plan was shaped by hope, not estimates. Someone picked a date or a number, and the scope was expected to fit.

**How to avoid it:**
- Get estimates from the people doing the work
- Include testing, project management and a buffer of 15 to 25 percent
- Be honest about trade-offs: scope, time and cost can't all be fixed
- Use a paid discovery phase to improve estimates before committing

## 4. Poor communication

**What happens:** the client and the team assume different things. Messages get lost, decisions are slow and surprises appear late.

**How to avoid it:**
- Name one decision-maker on your side
- Hold a short weekly meeting and a demo every one to two weeks
- Use a shared task board that everyone can see
- Write decisions down
- Raise concerns early, even if they're awkward

## 5. Slow feedback and decisions

**What happens:** the team waits for answers, access, content or approvals. Days become weeks, and the schedule slips for reasons the team can't control.

**How to avoid it:**
- Agree on response times, for example one working day
- Prepare content and access before the project starts
- Make sure the decision-maker has time and authority
- Treat client delays as a real project risk

## 6. Building without users

**What happens:** the product is designed from assumptions. It launches, and users don't want it, can't use it or don't see the point.

**How to avoid it:**
- Talk to real users before you build
- Test clickable prototypes before development
- Release early to a small group
- Measure actual use, not just opinions

## 7. Weak testing and quality control

**What happens:** bugs appear late, often after launch. Trust erodes, and fixing them costs far more than finding them early.

**How to avoid it:**
- Make sure the plan includes testing at every stage, not just the end
- Use automated tests for critical flows
- Test on real devices and browsers
- Have someone other than the developer check the work
- Reserve time for fixes before launch

## 8. Wrong team or wrong partner

**What happens:** the people lack the right skills, or the agency sells well and delivers poorly. Key people leave, and knowledge goes with them.

**How to avoid it:**
- Check recent, relevant work and talk to past clients
- Meet the people who'll actually do the work
- Ask how they handle problems, not just success
- Insist on documentation and shared code ownership

## 9. Technical shortcuts and neglected foundations

**What happens:** the team rushes, skipping structure, documentation and security. Early speed turns into slow, fragile software later.

**How to avoid it:**
- Agree on quality standards at the start
- Allow time for clean code and basic documentation
- Review security before launch
- Pay down technical debt regularly, instead of letting it pile up

## 10. No plan for life after launch

**What happens:** the project ends at delivery. Nobody owns the product, updates stop, bugs linger and the software decays.

**How to avoid it:**
- Budget for maintenance, around 15 to 25 percent of the build cost per year
- Name an owner for the product
- Plan hosting, monitoring and support before launch
- Have a roadmap for improvements

## 11. Lack of executive or owner support

**What happens:** the project has no champion. Priorities shift, staff don't adopt the new system and decisions stall.

**How to avoid it:**
- Make sure a senior person owns the outcome
- Involve the people who will use the software from the start
- Plan training and change management, not just the software

## Early warning signs

Catch trouble early. Watch for:

- Demos that keep slipping or show little progress
- Vague answers about status
- Growing lists of "known issues"
- The team avoiding bad news
- Scope that changes weekly
- Budget used faster than work completed
- Key people leaving
- You can't explain what's been built

If you see two or more, stop and reset. Ask for a frank review, and agree on what to cut, fix or change.

## What to do if your project is in trouble

1. **Pause and assess.** What is built, what works and what remains?
2. **Reconfirm the goal.** Is it still the right one?
3. **Cut scope.** Focus on the smallest valuable version.
4. **Get an independent review** of the code, plan and estimates.
5. **Reset expectations** on time and cost with stakeholders.
6. **Decide: fix, restructure or restart.** Don't let sunk cost drive the decision.
7. **Secure your assets.** Make sure you have the code, accounts and documentation.

## A healthy project looks like this

- Clear goals everyone can repeat
- A small first release
- Working software every one to two weeks
- Honest, regular communication
- A visible list of tasks and risks
- Testing built in
- Changes handled through a simple process
- A plan for after launch

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the main reason software projects fail?
Unclear goals and uncontrolled scope. When nobody agrees what success means, effort spreads in many directions.

### How can I tell if my software project is in trouble?
Missed demos, vague status updates, growing bug lists, budget use that outpaces progress and constantly changing scope are common signs.

### Why do software projects go over budget?
Usually because scope grew without adjusting the budget, estimates were too optimistic or rework was needed from poor testing and unclear requirements.

### Can a failing project be saved?
Often yes. Reduce scope, get an independent review, reset the plan and focus on a smaller valuable release.

### Does agile prevent failure?
It helps, by delivering in small steps and exposing problems early. But it still needs clear goals, engaged owners and honest communication.

### How do I reduce the risk of a new project?
Start with discovery, build a minimum version, hold regular demos, test early, agree on a change process and plan for after launch.
