Here are 15 questions that separate strong partners from risky ones, with what a good answer sounds like.

## About their experience

### 1. Have you built something similar to this?
**Good answer:** a specific example, what was hard about it and what they learned. They can describe the problem, not just the screenshots.
**Warning:** "We can build anything." Everyone says it, and it means nothing.

### 2. Can I speak to two or three past clients?
**Good answer:** "Yes, we'll introduce you." Then ask those clients how problems were handled.
**Warning:** only written testimonials, no calls, or a long delay.

### 3. What types of projects are you not a good fit for?
**Good answer:** a clear answer. Honest teams know their limits.
**Warning:** "We're a fit for everything."

## About the team

### 4. Who will actually work on my project?
**Good answer:** names, roles and seniority. You meet at least the project lead and a developer before signing.
**Warning:** vague talk about "our team," or the people in the sales meeting disappear after signing.

### 5. What happens if a key person leaves?
**Good answer:** documentation, shared knowledge and a replacement plan with a handover period.
**Warning:** no plan, or "that never happens."

## About process and communication

### 6. How will I see progress?
**Good answer:** regular demos every one to two weeks, access to a task board and a named contact for questions.
**Warning:** progress reports only at the end, or "you'll see it when it's ready."

### 7. How do you estimate, and how accurate are your estimates?
**Good answer:** they break the work into parts, explain assumptions and give a range with confidence levels.
**Warning:** a single big number after one call.

### 8. What do you need from me?
**Good answer:** a clear list: decisions, content, access, feedback times. Good teams know that client delays slow projects.
**Warning:** "Nothing, just leave it with us." Projects that need no client input rarely fit the client's real needs.

## About quality

### 9. How do you test your work?
**Good answer:** they mention testing at several levels, real devices or browsers, security checks and a bug-fixing period after launch.
**Warning:** "We test it ourselves at the end."

### 10. How do you handle security and data protection?
**Good answer:** practical answers: access control, encryption, backups, secure storage of secrets, review of third-party tools and data handling aligned with relevant rules.
**Warning:** a blank look or only general statements.

## About money and contracts

### 11. What is included in the price, and what is not?
**Good answer:** a written scope with clear exclusions, such as content creation, third-party fees or extra rounds of design.
**Warning:** a number with no list.

### 12. How do you handle changes to the scope?
**Good answer:** a simple process. Each change is described, estimated and approved before work starts.
**Warning:** "We'll absorb it" (then the bill comes later), or "any change is a new project."

### 13. Who owns the code, the designs and the accounts?
**Good answer:** you do, from the moment you pay, and you get access to all repositories and accounts.
**Warning:** the agency keeps the code until a final payment, or hosts everything under its own account with no handover plan.

## About life after launch

### 14. What support do you offer after launch?
**Good answer:** a defined bug-fix period, optional maintenance plans with clear response times and a path for new features.
**Warning:** "Once it's live, we're done."

### 15. What would make this project fail, and how would we reduce that risk?
**Good answer:** a candid answer about scope creep, slow feedback, unclear goals or technical unknowns, with a plan to deal with each.
**Warning:** "Nothing. We never fail."

## How to use these questions

Ask them in a call, not by email. The tone matters as much as the answer. Notice whether the team:

- Answers directly or avoids the question
- Admits unknowns instead of bluffing
- Asks you good questions back
- Treats your concerns as normal, not annoying

After the call, write down your impressions the same day. After three or four calls, patterns appear quickly.

## Five questions to ask yourself before the call

The best conversations happen when you are clear on your side too.

1. **What problem am I solving, and for whom?** If you can't say it in two sentences, the agency can't build it.
2. **What does success look like in six months?** A number, a behavior or a result. Not "a great app."
3. **What is my real budget, and how firm is it?** Be honest. A sensible agency will propose the best first version for that number.
4. **Who on my side makes decisions?** One person with authority beats a committee.
5. **How much time can I give each week?** Most projects need a few hours of feedback and decisions every week.

If you can answer these, the agency can answer yours, and the quotes you get will be far more accurate.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the most important question to ask a software company?
Ask who will work on your project and how you will see progress. Many problems come from unclear ownership and missing updates.

### How do I know if a development company is trustworthy?
Look for specific case studies, reference calls, clear contracts and honest answers about risks. Trust is built through openness, not through confident sales language.

### Should I ask for a free prototype or sample?
It is reasonable to ask for a small, paid pilot or a discovery phase. Asking for large amounts of free work usually leads to rushed or recycled output.

### How many questions are too many?
There is no limit. A good agency welcomes questions, because informed clients make better partners.

### Should I ask for a fixed quote in the first call?
No. A responsible quote needs discovery. You can ask for a rough range, with the assumptions behind it.
