Outsourcing fails when the client steps back too far or the contract leaves them without leverage. Here is how to avoid both.

## What you keep and what you delegate

**You keep:**
- The business goals and the definition of success
- Priorities: what gets built first
- Approval of designs and finished work
- Ownership of code, designs, data and accounts
- Final decisions on budget and scope

**You delegate:**
- Technical design and implementation
- Day-to-day task management
- Testing and quality checks
- Deployment and infrastructure setup
- Technical documentation

Blurring these lines is how control disappears.

## Step 1: Get clear before you outsource

Write a short brief covering:

- The problem and the users
- The three to five most important features
- Your budget range and deadline
- Systems it must connect to
- Success measures, for example "customers can complete a booking in two minutes"

A vague brief produces vague proposals. It also leaves you unable to judge the result.

## Step 2: Choose the partner carefully

Look for:

- Relevant, recent work and clear case studies
- References you can call
- Named people for your project
- A clear process for planning, demos and testing
- Honest answers about risks and limits
- Willingness to share ownership of everything you pay for

## Step 3: Protect yourself in the contract

A fair contract protects both sides. Make sure it covers:

- **Ownership.** You own the code, designs and deliverables from the date you pay. Accounts, repositories and hosting are in your name.
- **Scope and exclusions** written clearly
- **Milestones and acceptance criteria.** What "done" means for each stage.
- **Payment schedule tied to delivery,** not just to dates
- **Change process.** How new requests are estimated and approved.
- **Confidentiality** of your idea, data and customers
- **Data protection** responsibilities and security expectations
- **Warranty period** for fixing defects after launch
- **Termination terms.** How either side can end the contract and what you receive when it ends.
- **Handover.** Code, documentation, credentials and a walkthrough on exit.
- **Replacement of key staff** and notice requirements

A short legal review is worth the cost, especially for cross-border contracts.

## Step 4: Control the accounts and code from day one

This is the most important practical safeguard.

- Create the **code repository** under your own account and give the vendor access
- Register **domains, hosting, app store and cloud accounts** in your name
- Store **passwords and keys** in a shared password manager that you control
- Keep **design files** in a workspace you own
- Require **regular backups** to a location you control

If the relationship ends, you should be able to hand everything to another team the same day.

## Step 5: Set up governance

Governance is a light structure that keeps the project visible.

- **One decision-maker** on your side, with time and authority
- **One project manager** on theirs
- **A weekly meeting** for status, risks and decisions
- **A demo every one to two weeks,** showing working software, not slides
- **A shared task board** you can see at any time
- **A monthly review** of budget, schedule and risks
- **A written log** of decisions and changes

Visibility beats trust alone. Good partners welcome it.

## Step 6: Manage scope and changes

- Keep a **backlog** of ideas separate from the current plan
- For each new request, ask: what does it cost, what does it delay and what does it replace?
- Approve changes in writing
- Review the budget against progress regularly

A simple rule: nothing is added without an estimate and your approval.

## Step 7: Check quality, not just progress

You don't need to read code to check quality. Ask for:

- **Demos on real devices** or a live test link
- **Test results** and a list of known issues
- **Automated tests** for the critical parts of the system
- **Code review** by someone other than the author
- **A security checklist** before launch
- **An independent review** at key milestones, for larger projects

A third-party technical advisor for a few hours a month can be a cheap insurance policy if you are not technical.

## Step 8: Build the relationship

Control is not only about contracts. It is also about trust and clarity.

- Treat the team as partners. Share context about your customers and goals.
- Give feedback quickly. Delays on your side stretch the project.
- Be honest about changes in priorities and budget.
- Say thanks, and flag problems early and calmly.
- Meet in person or on video regularly, especially at the start.

People do their best work when they understand why it matters.

## Step 9: Plan the exit from the start

Even if you never use it, an exit plan keeps the relationship honest.

- Keep documentation current: setup instructions, architecture notes and key decisions
- Make sure someone else could run the system if needed
- Schedule a handover rehearsal during larger projects
- Avoid single points of failure, such as one engineer who knows everything

## Common mistakes

- **Disappearing after signing.** Projects without client attention drift.
- **Vague scope.** It leads to disputes and extra charges.
- **No code ownership.** It creates lock-in.
- **Paying mostly upfront.** It reduces your leverage.
- **Skipping the demos.** You discover problems when it's too late.
- **Choosing on price alone.**
- **Not testing it yourself.** Use the product as a customer would.
- **Ignoring communication differences,** such as time zones and language.

## Working across time zones and cultures

- Agree on a **daily overlap window** of a few hours
- Use **written updates** so that nothing depends on a call
- Be clear and direct, and confirm understanding in writing
- Respect local holidays and plan around them
- Record important calls or share summaries

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I keep control of an outsourced project?
Own the goals, priorities and approvals. Control the code and accounts, require regular demos and pay against delivered milestones.

### Who should own the code?
You should, from the moment you pay. Put it in the contract and set up repositories under your account.

### How often should I check progress?
Review a demo every one to two weeks, and meet weekly. Longer gaps hide problems.

### Is it risky to outsource to another country?
It can be managed with a good contract, clear communication, references and ownership of your assets. Location matters less than process and people.

### What if the outsourcing partner stops delivering?
With your own accounts, documentation and recent code backups, you can move to another team quickly. That is the point of keeping control.

### Do I need technical knowledge to outsource?
Not necessarily, but an independent technical advisor helps you judge proposals and the quality of the work.
