Here is how the model works, what it costs and how to know whether it fits.

## How it works

You and the provider agree on a team: for example, three developers, one designer, one QA engineer and a part-time project manager. They work only on your product, join your meetings and follow your priorities. You pay a fixed monthly rate for the team, usually with a minimum commitment of three to six months.

**What the provider handles:** recruiting, salaries, equipment, HR, administration and often the day-to-day project management.

**What you handle:** direction. You set the priorities, answer questions, review work and make decisions.

It is closer to extending your company than hiring a vendor for a project.

## How it differs from other models

**Fixed-price projects** deliver a defined scope for a set price, and the team moves on. **Time and materials** bills hours for a project. **A dedicated team** is a standing group focused on your roadmap, billed monthly.

The difference is continuity. The same people learn your product and your users, and they get faster and sharper over time.

## When it works well

**You have a live product and a long roadmap.** Steady development, new features and improvements for a year or more.

**You need to scale fast without hiring.** Building an in-house team takes months. A dedicated team can start in weeks.

**You have a product owner.** Someone on your side who sets priorities, answers questions within a day and accepts or rejects work.

**Your requirements evolve.** The team adapts sprint by sprint instead of fighting a fixed scope.

**You value deep product knowledge.** The longer the team works with you, the less explaining you do.

**You want flexibility.** You can often scale the team up or down with notice.

## When it doesn't work

**Short, one-off projects.** The setup and the monthly cost don't make sense for a three-month website or a single feature.

**An unproven idea.** If you're still figuring out whether anyone wants the product, start with a discovery phase and a minimum version first.

**No one to lead from your side.** A dedicated team needs direction. Without a product owner, work drifts, and you pay the monthly fee anyway.

**A tiny budget.** Teams have a minimum viable size. If you can afford one developer part time, a freelancer or a small project is a better fit.

**You want a fixed price and no involvement.** This model assumes close collaboration.

## What to look for in a provider

- **You meet the team before signing.** Interview the engineers who'll work on your product.
- **Clear team structure.** Roles, seniority and time commitment in writing.
- **Replacement policy.** What happens if someone leaves, and how the knowledge is transferred.
- **Transparent reporting.** Weekly demos, a shared task board and clear velocity.
- **Your IP stays yours.** Code, designs and accounts belong to you from day one.
- **Flexible terms.** A reasonable minimum term and an easy way to scale up or down.
- **Time zone overlap.** At least a few hours of shared working time each day.
- **Security practices.** Access control, device policies and confidentiality agreements.

## How to make it work

1. **Appoint a product owner.** One person who sets priorities and answers questions fast.
2. **Start with a clear backlog.** Prioritized work for the first two to three sprints.
3. **Meet regularly.** A short daily check-in, a weekly demo and a planning session every sprint.
4. **Share context.** Give the team your customer feedback, analytics and business goals. Engineers build better when they understand why.
5. **Treat them as teammates.** Include them in chat and planning, not just ticket queues.
6. **Measure outcomes.** Track what the work achieves for users and revenue, not just hours.
7. **Plan the exit.** Keep documentation and code in your accounts, so you can bring the work in-house later.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the team like a vendor you only talk to monthly
- Changing priorities every few days
- Paying for a full team when there's only a half-team of work
- Not checking who's on the team after the sales process
- Skipping documentation, which creates dependency
- Not defining what success looks like

## Dedicated team vs in-house

| | Dedicated team | In-house |
|---|---|---|
| Time to start | Weeks | Months |
| Flexibility | Scale with notice | Slow to change |
| Recruiting and HR | Provider handles it | You handle it |
| Product knowledge | Builds over time | Builds over time, often deeper |
| Cost structure | Monthly fee | Salaries and overhead |
| Control | High, through direction | Highest |
| Best for | Fast growth and flexibility | Long-term core capability |

Many companies start with a dedicated team, then hire a core in-house group and keep the external team for extra capacity.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a dedicated development team?
A group of engineers and related roles who work only on your product for a monthly fee, managed day to day with your direction.

### How much does a dedicated team cost?
With us, roughly {{fact:price.team-starter-squad}} for a small team and {{fact:price.team-product-squad-from}} to {{fact:price.team-scale-team-to}} a month for a mid-size team, depending on the roles and seniority.

### Is a dedicated team better than outsourcing a project?
For ongoing product development, yes. For a defined, short project, project-based outsourcing is usually simpler and cheaper.

### How long does it take to set up a dedicated team?
Often two to six weeks, which is much faster than recruiting in-house.

### Who owns the code?
You should. Make sure the contract says so, and that all repositories and accounts are in your name.

### Can I scale the team up or down?
Usually yes, with notice. Check the contract for minimum terms and notice periods.
