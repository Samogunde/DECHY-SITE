Quotes go wrong when the information going in is thin. This guide shows how to prepare, what to ask for and how to compare what comes back.

## Why quotes are often wrong

Software is not a product you pick from a shelf. Two projects that sound the same can differ in cost by a factor of five, because details change everything: how many user roles, which integrations, what security level, how many screens.

Most bad quotes come from one of three causes:

1. **Missing information.** The agency fills the gaps with assumptions, and assumptions are wrong about half the time.
2. **Hidden exclusions.** The low price leaves out testing, design, hosting or support.
3. **Pressure to win.** Some agencies quote low to win the job and recover the difference through change requests.

You can avoid all three with preparation.

## Step 1: Write a one-page brief

You don't need a technical document. A clear page is enough. Include:

**The problem.** What is broken or missing today? Who suffers from it?

**The users.** Who will use the software? Customers, staff, partners, admins? Roughly how many?

**The goal.** What should be true when it works? Use a measurable target if you can: "reduce booking time from 10 minutes to 2" or "let customers pay online."

**The must-have features.** The five to ten things the product must do on day one.

**The nice-to-have features.** Things you'd like later. Mark them clearly, so they don't inflate the first quote.

**Systems it must connect to.** Payment providers, accounting tools, CRMs, existing databases, other apps.

**Platforms.** Web, iOS, Android, or a combination.

**Constraints.** Deadlines, legal rules, data location requirements, brand guidelines.

**Budget range.** Yes, share it. See below.

## Step 2: Share your budget range

Many people hide their budget, hoping for a lower quote. It usually backfires. Without a range, the agency can't tell whether you are thinking $10,000 or $200,000, so the quote is either padded or off target.

Share a range instead. "We have $30,000 to $45,000" lets the agency design the best first version for that money and tell you honestly if the goal doesn't fit the budget.

## Step 3: Gather what you already have

Anything that helps the agency see your project reduces the guessing:

- Sketches, wireframes or screenshots of apps you like
- Existing documents, spreadsheets or forms that show your current process
- Access to the current system, if you are replacing one
- A list of competitors or similar products
- Any designs or brand assets you already own

Even rough material beats a long email.

## Step 4: Ask for a structured quote

A good quote has clear parts. Ask for:

- **Scope of work**, listed in plain language, and an explicit list of what is not included
- **Assumptions**, such as "client provides all content" or "two rounds of design revisions"
- **Team and roles**, with time commitments
- **Timeline with milestones**
- **Price breakdown** by phase or feature area
- **Payment schedule** tied to milestones
- **Change process**, how new requests are priced and approved
- **Post-launch support** and warranty
- **Ownership terms** for code, design and accounts

A single number on a single page is not a quote. It is a guess with a logo.

## Step 5: Consider a paid discovery phase

If your project is bigger or less defined, ask for a paid discovery or scoping phase. Typically it takes one to four weeks and covers:

- User and stakeholder interviews
- Defining and prioritizing features
- Technical planning and architecture
- Clickable designs of the main screens
- A detailed estimate and roadmap

Discovery costs money, but it often lowers the total cost, because it removes surprises before the expensive part begins. It also gives you a plan you can take to other agencies if you decide to compare.

## Step 6: Compare quotes fairly

Put each quote into the same table.

When one quote is much lower, find out why. Usually something is missing: testing, a backend, an admin panel, project management or support. Ask the agency to confirm in writing what the lower price assumes.

## Questions to ask about any quote

- What are the three biggest risks to this estimate?
- Which parts are you least sure about?
- What would make the price go up?
- What could we cut to lower the price without hurting the core?
- How often do your projects go over the original estimate, and why?
- Who will work on it, and for how many hours a week?

Honest answers to these matter more than the number.

## Ranges are honest

Early on, you should expect a range, such as "$40,000 to $60,000," along with what moves the price within it. As scope firms up, the range narrows. A fixed price should come after enough definition to make it fair for both sides.

## Warning signs

- A precise price with no discovery or questions
- No written list of exclusions
- No testing or project management in the price
- Pressure to sign quickly
- Refusal to explain how the number was built
- Vague answers about who owns the code
- A price far below every other quote

## Frequently asked questions

### How accurate can a software quote be?
Early estimates are usually accurate within a range of 25 to 50 percent. After discovery, with clear scope, quotes can be much tighter.

### How many quotes should I get?
Three is usually enough. More than five becomes noise.

### Should I share my budget with agencies?
Yes, as a range. It helps agencies recommend what is realistic and prevents wasted time on mismatched proposals.

### Why do quotes differ so much?
Because of different assumptions about scope, team, testing, design level and support. Compare what is included, not only the total.

### Is a fixed-price quote better than an estimate?
Only if the scope is clear. A fixed price on a fuzzy scope often leads to disputes or cut corners.

### How long does it take to get a good quote?
For a clear project, a few days to two weeks. For a complex one, expect a discovery phase.
